About Bridge Pantry Chicago

A student-founded food recovery initiative connecting surplus food with neighbors who need it.

Our story

Bridge Pantry Chicago began in January 2026 with two volunteers, a borrowed car, and three to four boxes of unsold food collected each day from two quick-service restaurants.

We handed that food out to neighbors experiencing homelessness under bridges downtown and in Hyde Park. The idea was simple: restaurants throw away safe, prepared food at closing time, and people nearby need a hot meal. Collect the food before it is discarded and deliver it the same night.

It grew from two connected problems we kept seeing side by side: food insecurity among students and neighbors, and food waste across local restaurants. Today, eight Nando’s PERi-PERi locations release surplus to us on a weekly evening route from Hyde Park to Lakeview, and we also recover surplus from campus events at the University of Chicago.

We are a public-policy project as much as a service project. We measure what we recover, study what makes food recovery sustainable, and share what we learn so other organizations can do it too.

How we got here

A volunteer effort that has grown one partner at a time.

  1. January 2026

    Two volunteers start collecting unsold food from two quick-service restaurants and handing it out under bridges downtown and in Hyde Park.

  2. April 2026

    The Greater Chatham Initiative, a Chicago 501(c)(3), becomes our fiscal sponsor. Until then, the founders covered every cost themselves.

  3. 2026

    Eight Nando’s PERi-PERi locations join through the chain’s Harvest Program, giving us a predictable, branded supply of surplus across the central city.

  4. Next

    A pickup log and weighing at every stop, more partner restaurants, and a refrigerated pilot vehicle so we can reach more neighbors each night.

What guides our work

Five commitments shape every pickup, partnership, and policy idea.

  • Sustainability

    Safe food belongs on plates, not in landfills. Recovering it puts the water, labor, and energy already spent on a meal to work. We also count our own costs, including transportation, because credible impact means being honest about both sides.

  • Food justice

    Hunger is not shared evenly. In Chicago, food insecurity reaches 51% of South Side households and 61% of Black households (Greater Chicago Food Depository, 2026), and people living outside are poorly served by pantries built for families with kitchens. We bring meals to where people are.

  • Community empowerment

    Restaurants, students, volunteers, and neighbors each have a role. We keep jobs clear, shifts flexible, and decisions transparent so anyone can contribute.

  • Responsible food recovery

    We take safe, unsold food directly from approved providers, in sealed containers, and distribute it the same evening. We never accept leftovers from customers’ plates or partially consumed meals.

  • Public service

    We study the problem as well as respond to it. Our research proposes practical Illinois policy changes, from clearer donor guidance to edible-food recovery requirements for the largest food generators.

Mission

Bridge Pantry Chicago works to reduce food insecurity and food waste by connecting surplus food from local restaurants and food providers with students, families, and communities in need.

Through strategic partnerships, volunteer engagement, and responsible food recovery practices, we strive to build a more sustainable and equitable Chicago.

Vision

A Chicago where nutritious food is never wasted while community members have reliable access to the resources they need to thrive.

Muhammad Jibrail

FounderCurrent student, Harris School of Public PolicyUniversity of Chicago

Muhammad founded Bridge Pantry Chicago to address two connected challenges: food insecurity among students and community members, and food waste across local food establishments. He brings a public-policy lens to community service, pairing hands-on food recovery with research on how to make it financially sustainable and scalable.

He is the author of The Bridge Pantry: A Financially Sustainable Model for Reducing Food Waste and Homelessness in Chicago (2026), which tests the model with a cost-benefit analysis and proposes Illinois policy changes.

When I started handing out unsold restaurant food from a borrowed car, what struck me was the distance. Food headed for a dumpster and a person without a meal were often only blocks apart. Bridge Pantry Chicago exists to close that gap safely, reliably, and with dignity.

We are growing deliberately: measuring what we recover, partnering with the organizations already doing this work, and treating volunteers’ time as the valuable resource it is. Our goal is not to replace public food assistance or housing, but to complement them, and to make food recovery something Chicago can count on every night.

Muhammad Jibrail, Founder

How we operate

Trust is earned in the details. These are the standards we hold ourselves to.

  • Dignity first

    We do not photograph the people we serve without informed consent, and we report impact only in aggregate.

  • Complement, don’t replace

    Food recovery supports SNAP and housing programs; it does not substitute for them. We point neighbors toward public benefits.

  • Honest numbers

    We label estimates as estimates, publish our methods, and replace assumptions with measured data as we grow.

  • Fiscally sponsored

    Bridge Pantry Chicago is a fiscally sponsored project of the Greater Chatham Initiative, a 501(c)(3) nonprofit organization.
